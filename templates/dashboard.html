Replay a past decision · the outcome is known

Pick a date and the model returns the forecast it made at that moment — fitted only on data before it, with a five-day purge gap — and the outcome beside it. Two models cover this range: the licensed Baltic years to 24/07/2019, which score 64.9%, and the extended series after, which score 61.7%. Every call names the one that answered it and quotes that model's accuracy — there is no combined figure, because an average would describe neither. History starts in — although the panel begins in 2012: the earlier days trained the first fold, and a walk-forward model cannot grade the block it learned from. Showing a call for them would be marking its own homework.

Brent, copper, the dollar and owner equities are read from the data for this date — model inputs, not fields you fill in.

Direction accuracy by confidence · the model may stay quiet

A chartering desk fixes a handful of cargoes a quarter, so the number that matters is not the average call — it is whether the calls it acts on beat the ones it declines to make. Confidence here is the size of the prediction, which is known the moment the model runs and needs no outcome. The threshold for “the strongest half” is a quantile of earlier predictions only, so no week is ranked using anything from after it; the first — scored weeks are not eligible for that reason, and the always row is measured on the same eligible weeks, so the comparison is like-for-like rather than an artefact of dropping the burn-in.

What the forecast was worth · simulated procurement

Direction accuracy is a statistic; a steel producer buys freight. So: a cargo has to move, and the desk either fixes today or holds one forecast horizon and fixes then. Every figure is a share of the freight rate — the Baltic series here is an index in points, this repository has no sourced conversion to dollars, and inventing one would put a fabricated number at the centre of the result. Decisions are spaced a full horizon apart, so no market move is counted twice.

Where it works, and where it stops · every year, including the bad ones

One number over eight years invites exactly one question — does it still work? — and answering from the same average is not an answer. Split by calendar year, which nobody can accuse us of choosing. The two columns disagree, and that disagreement is the point: RMSE skill measures variance explained, so it falls apart in a calm market where there is little variance to explain and a few large misses dominate. Direction is scale-free. This repository called that fragility in writing before it measured it.

Control experiment

The Baltic Capesize index is a daily broker survey; panellists anchor on the previous print, so its returns are autocorrelated and genuinely forecastable. BDRY is a liquid, arbitraged ETF over the same underlying. We ran the identical method on it — scoring well there too would mean claiming to beat a futures market, which is a red flag rather than a feature.
